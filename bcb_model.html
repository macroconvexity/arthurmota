Brazil small model

Research lab · Monetary policy

A four-equation semi-structural model of Brazil, in the style of the small aggregate model the Banco Central do Brasil describes in its reports. Choose a starting point, apply shocks and read the paths of inflation, the output gap, the policy rate and the exchange rate.

How to read this

The model is a loop with four links. A policy rate above neutral cools demand (the output gap). Weak demand and a stronger currency slow inflation. Slower inflation pulls expectations down. The central bank sets the rate by looking at where expectations sit against the target. Each quarter the loop turns once.

The solid line in each chart is your scenario, shocks included. The dashed line is the same starting point with no shocks. The distance between the two is what the shock does. Every number on this page is a model output from the inputs you set. Nothing here is live data or a forecast.

Real rate is the policy rate minus expected inflation. Inflation in the tables is the four-quarter rate. When the target is above foreign inflation, as in the default settings, the exchange rate index drifts up in every path, because the model assumes the real loses value at the gap between the two. With the target equal to foreign inflation there is no drift.

Method and limits

Units. One period is a quarter. Inflation π is quarterly inflation at an annual rate, and the charts show its four-quarter average, π4. Rates are % a year. The output gap h is % of potential. The exchange rate change Δe is % per quarter, with e in reais per dollar. Before quarter 0, inflation is assumed flat at the starting value.

Expectations. Slow to move, pulled by past inflation and demand, anchored partly on the target π*.

πet = f1·πet−1 + f2·π4t−1 + f3·ht−1 + (1 − f1 − f2)·π*

IS curve. Demand responds with a lag to the gap between the real rate and the neutral real rate r*, and to the fiscal impulse g.

ht = b1·ht−1 − b2·(it−1 − πet−1 − r*) + b3·gt

Taylor rule. Two lags of the rate for smoothing, and a response to expected inflation against target. The monetary shock m is added once.

it = t1·it−1 + t2·it−2 + (1 − t1 − t2)·[r* + π* + t3·(πet − π*)] + mt

Exchange rate. A reduced form of uncovered interest parity: the real drifts at the inflation differential, gains when the rate rises against a fixed foreign rate, and takes the exchange rate shock x as a permanent level jump.

Δet = (π* − πf)/4 − d·(it − it−1) + xt

Phillips curve. Inertia, expectations, the exchange rate measured against its trend drift, demand, and the supply shock s. The weights on past and expected inflation add to one, so inflation settles at the target.

πt = a1·πt−1 + (1 − a1)·πet + 4·a2·(Δet − drift) + 4·a3·(Δet−1 − drift) + a4·ht + 4·st

drift = (π* − πf)/4

What the default calibration implies. Starting from steady state: a 10% depreciation adds about 0.8 points to 12-month inflation at the peak, four quarters out. A 100 basis point monetary shock grows to about 160 basis points through the smoothing terms, opens a gap of about −0.6 points and takes about 0.2 points off inflation. A supply shock of 1 point on the price level lifts 12-month inflation by more than 2 points at the peak, because inertia carries each quarter's inflation into the next. A fiscal impulse of 1% of GDP opens a gap of about half a point.

Limits. The coefficients are an illustrative calibration chosen to give plausible magnitudes. They are not the central bank's published estimates, and the bank's own model is richer: it separates market-set from administered prices, uses commodity prices and a country risk measure, and treats expectations as partly model-consistent. Here expectations are backward-looking, so announcements and credibility have no effect. The neutral rate, foreign inflation and the foreign interest rate are constants. The rule ignores the output gap and the policy rate cannot go below zero. The exchange rate has no risk premium that responds to fiscal policy. All relationships are linear, so a shock twice as large has twice the effect.

Educational tool. Illustrative inputs, not live data. The starting values are not the current Selic, inflation or exchange rate. Not investment advice.
