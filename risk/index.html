How to read this page

What a factor is. Stocks that share a trait tend to move together: small companies, cheap companies, companies that went up last year. A factor measures the return to that trait.

How it is measured. Each factor is a portfolio that buys the stocks with a lot of the trait and sells the stocks with little of it. Its return is the difference between the two sides:

factor return = return of the long side − return of the short side

Read it against zero, not against the index. Both sides hold stocks, so both rise in a bull market. A factor at zero means the two sides earned the same. A factor at minus 4% a year means the long side earned four points less than the short side, not that it lost money. Only MKT, the market itself, compares with the S&P 500.

What it is for. Factors are a measuring tool, not a strategy. Regress any fund or portfolio on them and the coefficients say where its returns come from: how much is the market, how much is a tilt to small, cheap or trending stocks, and what is left over.

Risk and return

CAGR geometric. Sharpe and Sortino on excess returns over the T-bill, annualised; Sortino uses downside deviation. t is the t-statistic of the mean excess return. VaR and CVaR are historical, 95%, one day. Beta and information ratio are against the S&P 500. Factor rows are zero-investment spreads, so Sharpe uses them as is.

Factor regressions

Each row is a portfolio explained by the factors. The regression is

Rp − Rf = α + β1·MKT + β2·SMB + β3·HML + … + ε

A beta of 1 on MKT means the portfolio moves one for one with the market. A positive beta on SMB means it behaves like small companies, on HML like cheap ones, and so on. Alpha is the return the factors do not explain. R² is the share of daily movement they do explain.

Limits

Survivorship bias, the main limit. The model holds companies that exist today, so companies that failed or were delisted are missing. This flatters long-only portfolios, small caps most of all, and it works against factors that short risky stocks (IVOL, MAX, QMJ, BAB), because the worst outcomes on the short side are absent. Use the sorted portfolios for exposures, not for the level of return. Long-short factors are less affected because both legs share the bias.

Check against the academic library. The table compares each factor with its Ken French counterpart as a diagnostic. The definitions differ, so the match is not expected to be exact.
