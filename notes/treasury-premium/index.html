Result

The Treasury premium here is the SOFR swap rate minus the Treasury yield at the same maturity. A positive number means investors accept a lower yield on the Treasury than on the swap, which is what a convenience yield looks like. A negative number means the Treasury has to pay more than the swap.

Against the G10

The other standard measure compares the Treasury with G10 government bonds swapped into dollars (Du, Im and Schreger, 2018). It is built from cross-currency swaps that have no public source, and the published series stops in June 2025. The two measures are linked by an identity: the hedged measure equals the US premium over swaps, minus the foreign bond's own premium over its swaps, minus the cross-currency basis.

Limits

  • A swap is not a bond. It needs no funding and little balance sheet, so part of the gap is the cost of holding a cash bond and not a judgment on the Treasury as a safe asset.
  • Treasury par yields are the Treasury's fitted curve and the swap rates are an exchange's end-of-day curve. The two are struck at different times of the afternoon, which adds noise of a few basis points on volatile days.
  • The record starts in October 2020, when a complete SOFR curve becomes available. Comparisons with the years before 2020 need Libor swaps, which carried bank credit risk and are not comparable.
  • The 3-month and 6-month readings move with Treasury bill supply and with the path of policy priced inside the quarter, and are the noisiest on the page.
  • The hedged G10 series is read from its authors' dataset and ends on 30 June 2025. Nothing after that date is estimated here.
