How to read this page

The Treasury curve already contains a path for itself. A forward yield is the rate the market locks in today for a bond that starts at a later date, so the curve says where each maturity is priced to be next month, next quarter and at the end of next year. Federal funds futures do the same for the policy rate, meeting by meeting. This page reads both every day and lays them out month by month.

It then asks a second question: what rate would the Federal Reserve's own past behaviour call for? A set of forward-looking policy rules gives two numbers. The first is the rate the rules call for: where policy would sit if it adjusted in full. The second is the rule path: starting from today's rate and closing the distance at the pace the Federal Reserve has moved in the past, meeting by meeting. The gap between the rule path and the priced path moves the curve to give a second set of projections.

Priced is not predicted. A forward yield includes a premium for bearing risk, and since 1990 forwards have been a worse guide to where yields ended up than assuming no change (see the track record). The rules describe past behaviour, not what policy should be. Not investment advice.

Limits

Forwards carry a term premium, so the priced curve is a break-even, not a forecast. The futures path carries a small premium as well. The neutral rate behind any policy rule is not observed, and every rule inherits that uncertainty. A committee that reacts to something the rules do not see will depart from them. The rule numbers move less often than the priced path, which moves every day. The curve on the rule path ignores changes in the term premium.
